The basics

What does a currency heat map show?

Each cell compares two currencies. The percentage shows how much that pair moved over the selected timeframe. It does not measure how good a currency looks on its own. A currency can rise against the dollar and still fall against the euro.

For example, if EUR gains 1.2% against USD in a week while USD gains 0.4% against JPY, EURUSD turns green because the euro outperformed. If both currencies move by the same amount, the cell stays flat. The map measures relative strength, not absolute direction.

How do you use it in practice?

  1. Pick a timeframe that matches your trade: 1 day for intraday, 1 week for swings.
  2. Compare the strongest and weakest currency on the same row or column.
  3. Check open and planned positions for the same exposure before you add risk.

Three things this map cannot do

  • It cannot predict where price goes next. It reports what already happened.
  • It cannot show volume or order flow, so it is not a liquidity map.
  • It cannot replace your stop loss or your position size.

Three windows

Which timeframe should you use?

Use 1 day for intraday bias, 1 week for swing confirmation and 1 month for trend context. The same pair can be green on one timeframe and red on another. Each window describes a different move, so neither colour is wrong.

1 Day

Intraday momentum

Useful for today's session bias. News-heavy and noisy, so a green daily cell can reverse by London close. Treat it as a filter, not a plan.

1 Week

Swing confirmation

A whole trading week of evidence. Strength here is more trustworthy than a single daily spike, and it is the window most swing traders watch.

1 Month

Trend and reversion

A longer look at which currencies are genuinely trending. Extreme readings can also highlight stretched moves that are candidates for mean reversion.

The grid uses daily ECB reference rates, not a tick feed. A cell reflects the comparison dates shown under it.

Data

Where does the heat map data come from?

The grid uses European Central Bank reference rates, fetched through the Frankfurter API. The ECB publishes these rates once each business day, usually around 16:00 CET. The page loads the latest set when you open it, and the reference date appears under the grid.

It is not a broker quote and not a tick feed. Your platform's bid and ask will differ. The request is made from your browser to Frankfurter, and no account data is sent.

Risk first

How do you avoid stacking correlated trades?

Check correlated positions before you enter. Long EURUSD, long GBPUSD and long AUDUSD are one short USD position, not three separate trades. A single dollar move can hit all three at once.

Use the heat map before you stack. If your candidates are all green on the same side, they are probably correlated. If they also move together on the weekly view, the correlation is structural, not a one-day coincidence.

Cap total exposure per currency

Three 1% trades that all lose when the dollar strengthens is a 3% loss in one move. Add them up before you enter.

Size the combined trade, not each ticket

Split your risk budget across correlated entries instead of applying the same lot size to each. The lot size calculator does the maths per trade.

Diversify by currency, not by symbol

USD risk, JPY risk and commodity-currency risk are different exposures. Holding three symbols that all depend on one driver is not diversification.

If three positions share one driver, size the group as one trade.

Answers

What else should you know about the currency heat map?

The questions below cover what the grid shows, where the data comes from and what it cannot tell you.

What is a currency heat map?

+

A grid that compares currency pairs and colours them by percentage change over a period. Green shows the base currency outperforming the quote; red shows underperformance. It is a relative-strength view of the forex market.

What do the colours mean?

+

Deeper green means stronger outperformance, deeper red means stronger underperformance, and grey means the two currencies moved roughly together. The percentage in each cell is the pair's move over the selected timeframe.

Is the data live?

+

The page loads European Central Bank daily reference rates when you open it. You can refresh the data at any time. It is not a tick-by-tick feed, so use it for session bias and swing analysis, not scalping.

Can it predict where a pair will go?

+

No. It describes relative strength over the past day, week or month. The grid is a filter for momentum and correlation, not a forecast, a signal service or a liquidity map.

Why do some pairs barely change colour?

+

Because the two currencies moved by nearly the same percentage. Strength is relative: EUR and USD can both rally hard and still leave EURUSD almost unchanged.

Which currencies are included?

+

Eight majors: EUR, USD, GBP, JPY, AUD, CAD, CHF and NZD, which gives 56 pair comparisons. Exotic pairs and metals are outside this grid.

Is there a gold or liquidity heat map?

+

No. This is a currency-only heat map, and it does not measure order-book liquidity. A liquidity heat map shows resting orders at price levels, which is a different dataset. For gold position sizing, use the lot size calculator.
